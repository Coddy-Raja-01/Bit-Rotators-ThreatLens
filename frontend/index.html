Case Study: The eMudhra TLS Certificate Key-Generation Flaw

Factual summary based on public security disclosures and CA/Browser Forum reports (2022–2023).

In 2022 and 2023, independent security researchers auditing public Certificate Transparency (CT) logs discovered a critical flaw in TLS certificates issued by eMudhra, a commercial Certificate Authority based in India. The weakness originated in an automated certificate enrollment and key-generation process that relied on flawed or insufficiently seeded pseudo-random number generation (PRNG).

The Mathematical Vulnerability (Shared Prime Factors):
RSA cryptography relies on the mathematical difficulty of factoring a large composite modulus n = p * q into its secret constituent prime factors p and q. If the random number generator is defective, two distinct certificates issued to completely different domains may accidentally share one prime factor (e.g. p). Using the classical Euclidean algorithm to compute the Greatest Common Divisor:

gcd(Modulus_A, Modulus_B) = Shared Prime Factor p

A passive eavesdropper reading Certificate Transparency logs can compute the GCD in milliseconds on a standard laptop, instantly deriving the private keys for both certificates without breaking RSA mathematically. This allows total TLS interception, decryption of user sessions, and transparent impersonation.

Why Certificate Validation Matters:
This incident underscores that having an active HTTPS padlock is not synonymous with trust. Continuous auditing of certificate chains, key generation quality, Certificate Transparency logs, and revocation status is required to protect internet users from compromised trust chains.

Understanding Typosquatting & Homoglyph Spoofing

Attackers exploit visual similarities between characters to fool human eyes:

  • Character Pairs: Using the letters rn (r followed by n) to mimic the letter m (e.g. rnicrosoft.com).
  • Numeric Substitutions: Replacing the letter o with the number 0 or l with 1 (e.g. paypa1.com).
  • Unicode Homoglyphs: Registering Internationalized Domain Names (IDN) using Cyrillic characters (like Cyrillic small 'а' U+0430) that render identically to Latin 'a' in browser address bars.
  • Keyword Squatting: Affixing words like -security, -auth, -portal, or -verify to trusted brand names.
Fake Recruitment & Advance-Fee Employment Scams

A growing vector where job seekers are contacted via Telegram, WhatsApp, or unsolicited email claiming to offer high-paying remote roles:

  • Directs victims to spoofed applicant portals designed to harvest identity documents, Social Security Numbers, and bank details.
  • Requests upfront fees for "onboarding software", "identity verification checks", or "home office equipment" payable via cryptocurrency or gift cards.
  • ThreatLens static inspection looks for keyword combinations such as daily payout lures, task-based investment claims, and external submission forms.
Postal Redelivery & SMS Smishing Lures

Fraudulent SMS messages claiming an overdue toll violation (E-ZPass, SunPass) or undelivered postal package (USPS, FedEx, DHL). The pages ask for a nominal fee (e.g. $1.50) to steal credit card numbers, CVV codes, and billing addresses.

API Authentication

Public endpoints (like looking up community reports) are open. Automated scans and programmatic lookups support authentication via an API Key:

Authorization: Bearer tl_live_xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx
POST /api/scan

Runs a full threat scan on the submitted URL and returns the unified report.

curl -X POST "http://127.0.0.1:8000/api/scan" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"url": "http://rnicrosoft-login.net"}'
GET /api/lookalike?domain={domain}

Inspects a specific domain against typosquatting, homoglyphs, and known brand names.

curl "http://127.0.0.1:8000/api/lookalike?domain=paypa1-update.com"
GET /api/reports

Lists community threat reports with optional query filters (?category=banking&verified_only=true).

curl "http://127.0.0.1:8000/api/reports?limit=10"

About ThreatLens

Built to combine threat heuristics, web security posture, certificate validation, and community intelligence in one place.

Design Philosophy & Architecture

Existing threat scanners are typically fragmented: one tool looks only at DNS, another checks headers, and third-party Certificate Authority dashboards operate independently. ThreatLens brings all four pillars into a single unified report.

To ensure clarity and high utility, the user interface rejects cosmetic gimmicks, marketing slogans, and unnecessary decoration. It prioritizes straightforward data presentation, plain language explanations, and verifiable evidence.

Important Legal & Technical Disclaimer

ThreatLens security scan results represent automated indicators and probabilistic heuristics derived from static inspection, domain comparison, and public certificate databases. They are designed as diagnostic aids for security analysts and internet users. A low risk score is not an absolute warranty of domain safety, and a high risk score does not represent a legal determination of criminality. Always verify identities independently before submitting sensitive credentials or financial information.
